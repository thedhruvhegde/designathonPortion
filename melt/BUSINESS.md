# Melt: who uses it, and how everyone makes money

## The two sides

### Sellers: students who are leaving
- **Who:** graduating seniors, students going abroad or home for the summer, and anyone switching dorms or moving off-campus. They're leaving with more than they can carry, and the deadline is fixed.
- **The pain:** no time to haggle or relist, no car, and nowhere to store things. In the last 48 hours everything becomes a dumpster problem, and on some campuses abandoned items cost students cleaning or removal fees.
- **What Melt gives them:**
  1. **Cash, earlier.** The melt curve rewards listing early. An $80 futon listed 3 days out sells for $40–60 instead of $0.
  2. **Zero effort.** One listing and one curve. No negotiating, no relisting, no "is this still available?" ghosting. The price does the work.
  3. **A guaranteed exit.** If nobody buys, it goes to a student who needs it, then onto the donation truck. It's never your problem again.
  4. **A donation receipt** from the partner charity for anything that ends up donated (in-kind, tax-deductible where applicable).

### Buyers: students who are arriving or staying
- **Who:** incoming freshmen and transfers (especially international students who arrive with two suitcases), grad students, summer interns and researchers, returning students, and the local community.
- **What Melt gives them:**
  1. **Prices that only go down.** Watch an item, or catch it when the claim gauge says the risk of losing it is higher than the savings from waiting.
  2. **Furnish a room for a fraction of new.** A Quince dining chair at $105 → $40s. A wool coat at $70 → $30s.
  3. **The Kindness Window.** Students with an opt-in need flag (Pell, food- or housing-insecure, international freshman, first-gen) get a private 2-hour first claim on everything that hits $0. It's free, matched fairly by Gale–Shapley, and not first-come-first-served.

### The third party who pays: the university
Move-out costs universities real money: extra hauling and dumpster pulls, landfill tipping fees, staff overtime, and furniture they have to clean out of rooms. They also have to report waste diversion and basic-needs support. Melt turns that cost line into a measurable sustainability and equity program.

## How Melt makes money (est.)
| Stream | Who pays | Price (est.) | Why they pay |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Campus license** | Res-life or sustainability office | ~$1.50 per bed per year | It reduces move-out hauling and tipping fees, provides waste-diversion and STARS-style reporting data, and powers the basic-needs program |
| **Sale fee** | Buyer, added at checkout | 8% of paid sales; **$0 on free and Kindness claims** | A fair, cheap price for something that would otherwise be lost |
| **Delivery & hold** | Buyer, optional | $12 dorm-to-dorm delivery for big items; $15 to hold a claim until August | The "no car, no storage" problem |
| **Brand recommerce** | Brands (e.g. a DTC brand like Quince) | Per verified resale or trade-in credit | Brands want verified second-life data and take-back credit, and a route to the next customer |
| **City "Melt Week"** | Municipal sanitation and sustainability departments | Event sponsorship or grant | Cities face the same dumpster-day problem at every lease turnover (Aug 1 / Sep 1) |

### One campus, one spring (illustrative)
- 8,000 residents × 25% list × 3 items = **6,000 listings**
- 55% sell at an average of **$25** → **$82,500** GMV, which at 8% is **$6,600** in fees
- License: 8,000 beds × $1.50 = **$12,000**
- Delivery and holds: ~600 × $13 ≈ **$7,800**
- **≈ $26k per campus per year.** The marginal cost is hosting and a truck day the university already runs.
- Students keep **~$82k** that would otherwise have gone in the dumpster, and need-flagged students receive the free 20%.

*Projections are illustrative. They're labelled est. in the deck, and the assumptions are stated.*

## Why it scales
The same engine works anywhere there's a **hard deadline** plus **too much stuff**: campus move-out, apartment lease turnover, office closures, festivals and conventions, and retail end-of-season stock. The melt curve is the deadline, made visible.
